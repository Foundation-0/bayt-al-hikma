Arabic Lexicon: pattern, neighbourhood, remainder.

Arabic builds words by pouring a root into a pattern. This study measures three things: how fixed that choice of pattern is, which words keep company with which roots, and how much of the root link survives in English.

01 // Wazn19,347 verbs. Forms I to X.Single-corpus descriptive

Roots keep their habits.

Wazn means the verb pattern, Forms I to X in the classical system. Form II often adds force or causation. Form VI often adds mutual action. The study asks whether a given root sticks to a few forms or spreads across all ten.

The measure is Renyi-2 concentration. In plain terms, it is the chance that two verbs drawn from the same root share a form. The value across 1,642 roots is 0.926980. Two shuffle tests (global, and matched by frequency band) both give p = 1/251, Holm p_adj = 4/251.

The scope matters more than the number. No OpenITI control text in this program carries form tags, so there is nothing to compare against. The certificate states this in so many words: within-root claim scope is single_corpus_descriptive_morphology, and form_tagged_openiti_control_present is false. Roots preferring fixed stems is expected in any language built this way. No cross-corpus or uniqueness claim is made.

02 // Neighbourhoods15 roots. 14 fields. 9 tests.Survives Holm

Company a word keeps.

Fifteen roots were grouped by hand into fourteen meaning fields. For each root, the study records which other words appear nearby, after removing the ten most frequent words in the corpus. Three scores (ctx_r2, ctx_jaccard, cooccur) then ask whether roots in the same field keep more similar company than shuffled verse slots do.

All nine tests survive. Eight score p = 1/251 or 2/251, and the family holds under Holm-Bonferroni at p_adj = 9/251 = 0.0359. The odd and even halves agree, as split-half internal consistency checks.

The root list is the limit. Fifteen hand-picked roots can flatter any grouping. The locked protocol on the method page replaces hand-picking with a fixed frequency rule for any future confirmatory run. Until then, read this as a strong exploratory result on a named set.

03 // Translation defect131,343 verse pairs. 2 translators.Survives shuffle and distance

Same root, different word.

A lemma is a dictionary headword. One root can hold several lemmas. Take two verses that share a root. If they use the same lemma, English often repeats a word. If they use different lemmas of that root, English usually does not. The gap between those two cases is the projection defect: structure the Arabic shows and the English flattens.

Across 12 multi-lemma roots, the pooled gap is +0.3349 in Saheeh and +0.2734 in Pickthall. The same-lemma floor (pairs sharing no English word even with the same lemma) is 34.41% and 45.96%. Shuffling lemma labels inside each root 250 times never reaches the observed gap: p = 1/251 for both translators.

Distance is the obvious objection. Far-apart verses differ for many reasons. So pairs were matched in four verse-distance bands. The gap stays positive in all four bands for both translators, from +25 to +36 percentage points. Per root, the picture is honest about its edges: wzn fails Holm in both versions, and Erj fails in Pickthall. Eleven of twelve roots pass in Saheeh. Ten of twelve pass in Pickthall.

A positive gap is expected in any language where distinct lemmas carry distinct glosses. The contribution here is a measured size with stated controls, not a claim that Arabic alone behaves this way.
